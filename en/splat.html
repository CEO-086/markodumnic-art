A separate test

A work from every angle

A photograph of a canvas always lies about one thing: the light. Thick impasto and varnish change colour as soon as you move — a stroke that looks dark from one side flashes from the other. A single image cannot convey that.

So a single canvas is filmed with a phone from dozens of angles and turned into a Gaussian splat — a cloud of tiny soft points that records both the colour and the way the colour changes with the viewing angle. The result can be walked around like an object, rather than looked at like a picture.

This sits apart from the virtual exhibition room, and deliberately so — the reason is below, in the section on baked-in light.

This is where the work will appear, captured from every angle

The viewer is finished and working — only the capture is missing. Once the file is in place, this panel turns into a viewer you can move around. Until then, there is deliberately no borrowed capture here posing as one of Dumnić’s canvases.

  1. Capturing with a phone Hang the canvas on a wall or set it on an easel, in even light with no lamp reflected in the varnish. Move slowly around it in an arc, taking 40–80 photos or a short video, from below, level with and above the canvas. What matters is the angles covered, not the number of shots.
  2. Processing — Scaniverse The capture is processed in Scaniverse (Niantic) — free, on the phone itself, without uploading to a server, and it exports straight to SPZ. Processing takes anything from a few minutes to half an hour.
  3. Export to SPZ After processing, choose export to SPZ — the smallest of the supported formats. If SPZ is not offered, PLY, SPLAT or KSPLAT will also work; they are just noticeably larger.
  4. File to the site Save the file under the name of the work and place it in the splat/ folder at the root of the site:

splat/aria-di-torino.spz

Then add the file to the list in splat/index.json (see splat/README.md for how). Nothing else changes — neither this page nor the code. Each time it opens, the page reads that list and, as soon as the work is in it, offers a button to explore the work.

On file size: aim for under 15–30 MB. Beyond that, phones on a mobile network wait a long time and less powerful devices run out of memory. In Scaniverse the number of points can be reduced before export — for a single canvas, 1–2 million points is quite enough.

A limitation, stated plainly

The light is baked into the capture

A Gaussian splat does not record an object’s shape and colour separately. It records how the object looked — together with the light that fell on it that day. The reflection of a lamp in the varnish, the shadow of the frame, the warmth of the afternoon sun: all of it went into the capture and stays there for good.

That is why this work cannot be hung on a wall of the virtual room. The room has its own spotlights; had we brought the splat inside, the canvas would still carry the reflection from the studio, and the room’s spotlight would not even touch it. The canvas would seem to float in the wrong world.

This is not a fault in the viewer — it is how the technique itself works. Rather than hide it, the work stands here, in its own light, apart from the room.

  • Virtual room Flat images on the walls, lit by the room’s spotlights. The light changes as you move, but the surface of the canvas is smooth — there is no impasto and no varnish.
  • This page A single work, with its full surface: impasto, palette-knife marks, the sheen of varnish. But the light is the studio’s and does not change — which is why it stands alone, outside the room.
